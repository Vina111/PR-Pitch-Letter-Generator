# Product Media Pitch SOP

Use this reference when drafting, revising, validating, or planning product
launch outreach, especially for technology, health, lifestyle, consumer, or
review-driven products.

## 1. Product Research

Extract these facts from the user's materials before drafting:

- Product positioning and target audience: B2B, B2C, professional, consumer, or
  vertical audience.
- Features: what the product does.
- Benefits: why those features matter to the reader or buyer.
- Price, availability, launch timing, sample/review availability, and purchase
  path when provided.
- Competitors or substitutes.
- Technical parameters, patents, certifications, clinical review, regulatory
  status, or unique value proposition.
- Risky claims that need proof: biggest, fastest, first, best, most advanced,
  guaranteed, prevents, cures, predicts, or extends lifespan.

Do not invent missing facts. Ask for them or use bracketed placeholders.

### Story test (run before angle selection)

A hook is weak unless it clears at least three of these four:

- Change/timeliness: something is new, shifting, or expiring.
- Proof: a number, sample, study, named customer, or verifiable fact.
- Stakes: tension, cost, risk, or a winner/loser.
- Audience impact: a specific group in the target readership is affected, and
  you can say how.

A weak hook goes back to the user with 2-3 concrete strengtheners (new data
point, customer proof, trend peg, sharper audience) and the choice to
strengthen first or proceed anyway — per the skill's newsworthiness verdict.
Never draft past a weak hook silently.

## 2. Feature -> Pain Point -> Value

Convert product specs into a journalist-facing angle:

```text
Feature: what it does
Pain point: what problem the audience has
Value: why this makes life easier, safer, cheaper, clearer, faster, or more useful
```

Prefer reader value over company value. A pitch should not sound like a product
brochure.

## 3. Category And Article Analysis

When sources or tools are available, review competitor/category articles and ask:

- What do reporters repeatedly care about?
- What pain points appear in coverage?
- Which competitor benefits are already common?
- What differentiation can credibly exceed or complement the existing narrative?
- Is the best pitch a product launch, review invite, trend complement, scenario
  story, pain-point solution, or expert interview?

When sources are unavailable, frame this as a qualitative hypothesis, not a
reported finding.

## 4. Keyword And Trend Lens

Use Meltwater, MuckRack, Google, search tools, or user-provided data when
available to identify:

- Category keywords.
- Feature keywords.
- Pain-point keywords.
- Reporting trend: rising, falling, seasonal, event-driven, or evergreen.
- Outlets and journalist groups frequently covering related terms.
- Main sentiment and recurring reader questions.

Never invent volume, percentage growth, or trend numbers. If no trend data is
available, use plain relevance rather than fake metrics.

## 5. Angle Selection

Choose one primary angle from the reporter's point of view:

- Product launch or new availability.
- Solution to an industry or consumer pain point.
- Use-case or scenario angle.
- Technical or clinical differentiation.
- Market trend complement.
- Sample/review opportunity.
- Executive or expert interview.

For batch outreach, create separate versions by audience group instead of using
one generic pitch for everyone.

Regenerate/variant semantics: a request for "another version" means a
different angle from this menu — with fresh questions to the user for whatever
input the new angle needs — not a re-wording of the same thesis. Reserve
wording-only re-rolls for explicit requests.

## 6. Media List Logic

Prioritize journalists in this order:

1. Writers who covered direct competitors or the same product category.
2. Writers who covered umbrella terms around the category.
3. General technology, lifestyle, wellness, consumer, or gadget writers who fit
   the audience.
4. Use-case journalists whose beat matches the scenario, such as healthy aging,
   preventive health, family planning, executive health, remote work, education,
   travel, or small business.

For each contact, keep notes on outlet, role, recent relevant article, email,
why they fit, and the best pitch angle.

## 7. Pitch Letter Checklist

Run this checklist before final delivery:

- The subject is declarative, headline-like, and within ~9 words / 60
  characters.
- The first sentence gives the hook immediately.
- The pitch has 3-4 short paragraphs (unless the user opted into a licensed
  deviation such as ultra-short or data-first).
- The reader pain point and value are clear, and the why-it-matters paragraph
  names the affected reader community specifically.
- Technical details are translated into plain-language benefits.
- Only 3-4 strongest proof points are included.
- The source/spokesperson is credible and named when provided.
- The CTA asks for one low-friction next step.
- Reporter personalization is real or absent: one real recent piece with its
  date, a bracketed placeholder, or no personalization at all — never simulated
  familiarity, and no flattery openers ("I loved your article", "big fan",
  "came across your piece").
- No AI-tell vocabulary: "excited to share", "revolutionary", "game-changing",
  "cutting-edge", "delve", "seamlessly", "in today's fast-paced world".
- No bullets, inline links, attachment mentions, unsupported superlatives, or
  invented claims.
- The pitch is under 150 words unless the user explicitly asks for a longer
  format.

## 8. Outreach Rounds

Vary the angle by round:

- Round 1: strongest top-tier or most timely product/trend angle.
- Round 2: broader Tier 2 outreach with practical reader value.
- Round 3: vertical, scenario, review, or expert-commentary follow-up.

Do not send the identical pitch repeatedly when a different angle would better
fit the journalist group.
